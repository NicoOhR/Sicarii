The tech sector is incoherent as a product class, the string which ties together
unrelated corporations that are regarded as the technology sector is primarily
a cost curve unique to it. This is not an interesting observation; the product
of *all* technology companies is *not* code. To be clear I am not claiming that
"coding was never the hard part". What I want to establish before a more
quantitative breakdown is that the technology is always the means and never the
end. Google and Meta function as advertisiment/data brokerage conglomerates,
Kalshi and Polymarket are gambling bookies without the hats, Uber's primary
product is labor arbitrage with two Kafka instances deployed.

The members of the tech sector which do not sell to consumers directly and
market to other businesses instead are arguably the parts of the industry which
truly sell technology as a product. Even then, the B2B SaaS model relies on some
underlying asset that can be capitalized. For example, Salesforce owns the
digital real estate where customer data lives; it is a landlord business, and
its pricing reflects switching costs rather than the quality of the technology.
This is to say that the actual value proposition of the technology sector is not
how good their software is, but their ability to leverage a particular class of
asset. What unites these companies is that the assets which they trade on
require a high fixed cost and a marginal cost of replication, which produces
similar strategic problems for the cohort. It is conceptually incorrect to
analyze the balance sheets of any company classified in tech in the same way one
would analyze any other sector, except for maybe the financial sector, keep that
in mind. These companies sell different products to different customers and on
completely different time tables, and is only coherent on the level of capital
structure.

Hardware companies, which are often folded into the tech sector a la ETFs like
QQQ or XLK, make even the capital structure relation dubious. Each piece of
hardware produced retains the high fixed cost of design and fabrication
facilities, and also has a significant marginal cost. However, in this regard,
Nvidia is the exception which makes the rule; Nvidia is fabless, they invest in
the high fixed cost of research and investment and license design to
manufacturers cheaply, their moat is switching cost from their industry standard
CUDA ecosystem. Nvidia has become the engine behind the massive capex cycle that
the market has seen in the last two years. Their customers, Google, Meta,
OpenAI, etc. absorb Nvidia's output in an attempt to accelerate their own profit
drivers, Nvidia in turn acts as the guarantor of their customers purchases, as
well as customers in and of themselves to the same companies. This report seeks
to test if NVIDIA's reinvestment into it's own customers overstates the quality
of the reported earnings, and if current market evaluation depends on that
exaggeration.
